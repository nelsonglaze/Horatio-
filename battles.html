Engagements, 1780–1805

The battles

By his own count Nelson was under fire more than a hundred times. Four of those days were fleet actions that changed the war, and each has an animated plan here, drawn from above like the charts made for the Admiralty afterwards.

The four great actions

  • 14 Feb. 1797
    37°N 9°W     Cape St Vincent Jervis, with fifteen ships, cuts a Spanish fleet of twenty-seven in two. Nelson, a commodore in the 74-gun Captain, wears out of the line to head off their escape and takes San Nicolás and San Josef by boarding. Four prizes. Animated plan
  • 1–2 Aug. 1798
    31°20′N 30°07′E     The Nile Nelson's first fleet command. He finds Brueys at anchor in Aboukir Bay and attacks at dusk on both sides of the French line. Nine ships taken, the 120-gun Orient blown up, Bonaparte's army stranded in Egypt. Animated plan
  • 2 April 1801
    55°42′N 12°37′E     Copenhagen Sent under Hyde Parker to break the Armed Neutrality of the North, Nelson attacks the moored Danish line up an unmarked channel, ignores the recall, and negotiates the truce himself. Animated plan
  • 21 Oct. 1805
    36°17′N 6°15′W     Trafalgar Twenty-seven ships in two columns against Villeneuve's thirty-three. The Combined Fleet loses eighteen on the day. Nelson is shot from the Redoutable's mizzen-top and dies three hours later. Animated plan

How a sea battle under sail worked

Enough to follow the plans. Every term has an entry in the glossary.

The gun and the broadside

A ship of the line carried her guns along her sides, in two or three decks. She could fire almost nothing straight ahead or astern. To hit an enemy she had to turn her side to him, and to be hit least she wanted to face him end-on. Nearly everything in the tactics of the age follows from that.

The line of battle

The safe answer was to sail in a single line, each ship following the next, so that every broadside bore and every vulnerable bow and stern was covered by a friend. Two fleets in line would pass on parallel courses and pound each other. It was orderly and it rarely decided anything: a line that was getting the worst of it could simply bear away.

The weather gage

A ship could not sail closer than about six points, or sixty-seven degrees, to the wind. The fleet to windward, holding the weather gage, chose when and whether to fight; the fleet to leeward could only wait or run. The wind arrow on each plan is the first thing to look at.

Raking

A broadside fired through an enemy's bow or stern travelled the length of her gun decks, with nothing to stop it but men and guns. Victory's first broadside at Trafalgar, through the stern windows of Bucentaure, is said to have put four hundred men out of action. Crossing an enemy's stern was the best position there was.

Breaking the line

To steer through a gap in the enemy's line raked the ship on either side, cut off everything astern of the gap from everything ahead, and brought on a close mêlée in which rate of fire told. British crews, at sea all year, could fire about three broadsides to their opponents' two. Nelson's battles are four variations on finding or making that gap.

Striking

A ship surrendered by hauling down her colours. Firing on a ship that had struck was murder; firing after striking was treachery. The plans show a ship that has struck in outline.

Rates

Ships were rated by guns. A first-rate carried a hundred or more on three decks, like Victory. A third-rate of 74 guns on two decks was the standard ship of the line and made up most of every fleet. Frigates, with one gun deck, scouted and carried messages and kept out of the line.

What the guns fired

Round shot of 32, 24 or 12 pounds to smash hulls; chain and bar shot to cut rigging; grape and canister against men. The carronade, a short gun throwing a heavy ball a short way, was a British speciality. Victory had two of 68 pounds on her forecastle.

About the plans

They are reconstructions from the standard accounts. Positions, courses and times are approximate, and where sources disagree the plan follows the commonest version. Ship sizes are exaggerated so they can be seen. See the sources.
