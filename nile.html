Battles  ·  1–2 August 1798

The Battle of the Nile

At sunset, after hunting Bonaparte's fleet for ten weeks, Nelson finds it anchored in Aboukir Bay and attacks at once. His ships pass inside as well as outside the French line. By morning eleven of thirteen French ships of the line are taken or destroyed and a French army is marooned in Egypt.

Ten weeks of looking

In May 1798 a French armament was known to be fitting out at Toulon: thirteen of the line, some four hundred transports, 35,000 soldiers and General Bonaparte. Nobody in London knew where it was going. Lord St Vincent, off Cádiz, sent his most junior rear-admiral into the Mediterranean with three ships to find out.

Nelson began badly. On 20 May a gale off Sardinia took out Vanguard's foremast and topmasts and scattered his frigates, which went back to Gibraltar believing the flagship must have done the same. Alexander towed Vanguard clear of the rocks; she was re-rigged at sea in four days. The same gale let Bonaparte out of Toulon unseen. Reinforced to fourteen ships, Nelson now had to search an entire sea with no scouts. “Were I to die this moment,” he wrote, “want of frigates would be found stamped on my heart.”

He reasoned his way to Egypt, crowded on sail, and reached Alexandria on 28 June to find the harbour empty. He had passed the French in the night south of Crete and beaten them there by a day. Sick with the thought that he had guessed wrong, he doubled back to Sicily to water at Syracuse, then turned east again. On 1 August the masthead of Zealous saw Alexandria full of French transports, and at a quarter to three in the afternoon signalled thirteen sail of the line at anchor in the bay beyond.

Nelson, who had hardly eaten or slept for days, ordered dinner. “Before this time tomorrow,” he said as he rose from the table, “I shall have gained a peerage or Westminster Abbey.”

Why Brueys thought he was safe

Brueys had moored his fleet in a line a mile and a half long, close to the shoals that fringe the bay, with his heaviest ships in the centre. He believed nothing could pass between his leading ship and the shoal, or between his line and the shore. It was six in the evening; no admiral, he judged, would take a fleet into an uncharted bay with night coming on. A third of his men were ashore digging wells. The guns on the landward side were not cleared, and in some ships were blocked with stores.

There were three flaws. The ships were 160 yards apart, wide enough to sail through. They lay to single anchors by the bow, so each needed room to swing, and where a French 74 could swing a British 74 could float. And the wind blew straight down the line from van to rear, so the rear could not come up to help the van.

No signal for the decisive move

Nelson had talked through every case with his captains during the chase. His only tactical signals as the squadron stood in were to prepare to anchor by the stern and to attack the enemy's van and centre. Nobody signalled to pass inshore. Thomas Foley, leading in Goliath with the only decent chart in the squadron, saw the opening as he crossed Guerrier's bows and took it; four ships followed him. Nelson, sixth in the line, took Vanguard down the outside. The first eight French ships were caught between two fires, and the British worked methodically down the line from there.

“Victory is not a name strong enough for such a scene.”
Nelson, on the morning of 2 August 1798

Anchoring by the stern

Each British ship had a cable led out of a stern port. Dropping that anchor stopped her with the wind still behind her, beam-on to her chosen opponent, without the minutes of swinging that left a ship open to raking fire.

The night, hour by hour

  1. 2.45 pm

    Zealous signals the enemy fleet at anchor. Nelson recalls Alexander and Swiftsure from Alexandria and hauls up for the bay.

  2. 5.30 pm

    Signal to form line of battle “as most convenient”. Ships fall in as they come up; nobody waits. Culloden, towing a prize wine-brig, is seven miles astern.

  3. 6.20 pm

    Guerrier and Conquérant open fire. Ten minutes later Goliath crosses Guerrier's bows, rakes her, and anchors inside the line.

  4. 6.40 pm

    Vanguard anchors outside Spartiate at eighty yards. Culloden, cutting the corner in the last of the light, runs hard on the shoal off Aboukir Island.

  5. 7.00 pm

    Dark. British ships hoist four lanterns in a horizontal line at the mizzen peak so they can tell each other from the enemy. Bellerophon brings up alongside the 120-gun Orient.

  6. 7.30 pm

    Brueys, already twice wounded, is nearly cut in two by a round shot. He refuses to go below: a French admiral should die on his quarterdeck. He does, a quarter of an hour later.

  7. 8.00 pm

    Bellerophon, all three masts gone, drifts away down the bay. Alexander and Swiftsure arrive and take her place on either quarter of the flagship.

  8. 8.30 pm

    A piece of langridge lays Nelson's forehead open; the flap of skin falls over his good eye. “I am killed. Remember me to my wife.” In the cockpit he refuses to be seen before his turn.

  9. 9.00 pm

    Fire is seen in Orient's cabin. Swiftsure's gunners are ordered to aim at the flames. Nelson has himself led on deck to watch, and orders Vanguard's one undamaged boat away to pick up survivors.

  10. 10.00 pm

    Orient blows up. Blazing wreckage falls on Alexander, Swiftsure and Franklin. For several minutes nobody fires. About seventy of her thousand men are saved.

  11. Midnight

    Franklin strikes. The battle sinks into an exhausted lull, broken by bursts of fire until three in the morning.

  12. Dawn

    Six French ships have struck and Orient is gone. Theseus, Goliath, Alexander and Leander go down the bay; the grounded Heureux and Mercure strike.

  13. Noon

    Villeneuve makes sail with Guillaume Tell, Généreux and the frigates Diane and Justice and gets clear. Tonnant, dismasted, holds out until the 3rd; Timoléon is burnt by her crew.

What the Nile changed

No sea battle of the age of sail had ended so completely. Of thirteen ships of the line, nine were prizes and two destroyed. Bonaparte's army, which had won the Battle of the Pyramids ten days earlier, was cut off from France. He abandoned it a year later and slipped home in a frigate. The Mediterranean, from which the Royal Navy had withdrawn entirely in 1796, became a British sea again; Turkey, Russia, Austria and Naples joined a new coalition against France.

The news took two months to reach London, because Généreux, escaping, met and captured Leander with Captain Berry and the dispatches on board. When it came, the country went wild. Nelson became Baron Nelson of the Nile and of Burnham Thorpe, with a pension of £2,000 a year. The East India Company voted him £10,000. The Sultan sent a chelengk, a plume of diamonds with a clockwork centre that turned, to wear in his hat. Captain Hallowell sent a coffin made from Orient's mainmast, “that when you are tired of this life you may be buried in one of your own trophies”. Nelson kept it upright behind his chair in his cabin, and was buried in it.

He thought a barony mean, and he was probably right: Jervis had been made an earl for taking four ships. The reason given was that he had not been commander-in-chief.

A band of brothers

The phrase is Nelson's, borrowed from Shakespeare's Henry V, and he used it of the fifteen captains at the Nile. “I had the happiness to command a band of brothers,” he wrote to Lord Howe. Their portraits are on the captains page.

The boy on the burning deck

Orient's captain, Luc-Julien-Joseph Casabianca, had his young son Giocante serving aboard. Both were seen in the water clinging to a mast before the explosion; neither survived. Felicia Hemans's poem of 1826, “The boy stood on the burning deck”, was learnt by heart in British schools for a century.

The treasure

Orient carried coin and plate taken from the Knights of St John at Malta seven weeks before, reckoned at £600,000, to pay for the Egyptian campaign. It went up with her. Divers have since recovered guns, coins and the ship's rudder from the bay.

The six that came home

Franklin became HMS Canopus and served until 1887. Tonnant and Spartiate both fought on the British side at Trafalgar. Guerrier, Heureux and Mercure were too shattered to move and were burnt in the bay.
