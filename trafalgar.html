Battles  ·  21 October 1805

The Battle of Trafalgar

Twenty-seven British ships of the line attack thirty-three French and Spanish in two columns, break their line in two places, and take or destroy eighteen of them without losing a ship. Nelson is shot at a quarter past one and dies knowing he has won.

Two years to reach one afternoon

In the summer of 1805 Napoleon had an army of invasion encamped at Boulogne and no way to get it across the Channel. His plan was for the fleets at Toulon, Cádiz, Rochefort and Brest to break their blockades, draw the British off to the West Indies, double back, and hold the Channel for the few days the crossing would need.

Vice-Admiral Pierre-Charles Villeneuve got out of Toulon in March, picked up Spanish ships at Cádiz and reached Martinique. Nelson, who had watched Toulon for nearly two years without once setting foot ashore, guessed right, crossed the Atlantic after him with ten ships, and frightened him back to Europe. Villeneuve fought an indecisive action with Calder off Cape Finisterre in July, lost his nerve for the Channel, and in August put into Cádiz.

Nelson had twenty-five days at home at Merton. He sailed from Portsmouth in Victory on 15 September and took over the fleet off Cádiz on the 28th, the day before his forty-seventh birthday. He kept his main body fifty miles out to sea, with only Blackwood's frigates watching the harbour, so that Villeneuve might be tempted to come out.

By October Napoleon had already marched the Boulogne army east against Austria. He ordered the Combined Fleet into the Mediterranean to land troops at Naples, and sent Admiral Rosily to replace Villeneuve. Villeneuve heard his successor was at Madrid. On 19 October, rather than be relieved in disgrace, he began to work his thirty-three ships out of Cádiz.

The Nelson touch

The plan Nelson explained to his captains over dinner in Victory's great cabin, and wrote down in his memorandum of 9 October, threw away the usual line of battle. A single line took hours to form and usually let a beaten enemy get away. He would instead attack in columns, at right angles to the enemy, cutting their line in two or three places. The enemy's van would sail on out of the fight and need a long time to turn back against the wind; by then his whole force would have been concentrated on their centre and rear.

It had a price. Ships coming on bows-first could not fire a gun in reply for the last half mile, and the leading ships would take the whole weight of the enemy's broadsides. Nelson accepted that, trusting to slack French and Spanish gunnery after years in harbour, and put his heaviest ships at the head of each column. He led one himself.

“It was like an electric shock. Some shed tears, all approved. It was new, it was singular, it was simple.”
Nelson to Emma Hamilton, on telling his captains the plan, 1 October 1805

What the plan left to chance

The memorandum ended with the sentence that mattered most on the day: “in case signals can neither be seen or perfectly understood, no captain can do very wrong if he places his ship alongside that of an enemy.” Once the columns struck there would be no control from the flagship, and Nelson did not want any. He made only three general signals after the fleet bore up.

How to read the plan

North is at the top. British ships are red, French blue, Spanish ochre; a gold outline marks a flagship. Dotted lines are wakes. A ship turns white with a broken outline when she strikes her colours.

The plan is a reconstruction. Logs kept in thirty-three different ships disagree about times by up to an hour, and nobody recorded exact positions in the smoke.

Sea terms are explained in the glossary.

Why “weather” and “lee”

The wind was in the west-north-west. Nelson's column was the more northerly, on the windward or weather side; Collingwood's lay downwind of it, to leeward.

The day, hour by hour

Times are those most often given; ships' logs differ.

  1. 5.50 am

    First light. The fleets sight each other about eleven miles apart, the Combined Fleet to the east, heading south for the Strait.

  2. 6.10 am

    Nelson signals: form order of sailing in two columns. Then: bear up and sail large on course east-north-east. Then: prepare for battle.

  3. 8.00 am

    Villeneuve orders his fleet to wear together and steer north, to keep Cádiz under his lee. The manoeuvre takes two hours in the light air and leaves the line bunched, bowed and in places two and three ships deep.

  4. 11.00 am

    Nelson goes below, kneels at his desk and writes his prayer and a codicil to his will, witnessed by Hardy and Blackwood, leaving Emma Hamilton and his daughter Horatia “a legacy to my King and Country”.

  5. 11.45 am

    “England expects that every man will do his duty.” Lieutenant Pasco asks leave to use expects, which is in the signal book, for Nelson's confides, which would have to be spelt out.

  6. Noon

    Fougueux fires the first broadside at Royal Sovereign. Collingwood's flagship, newly coppered, is far ahead of her column.

  7. 12.10 pm

    Royal Sovereign breaks the line astern of Santa Ana. “See how that noble fellow Collingwood carries his ship into action!” says Nelson.

  8. 12.20 pm

    Victory comes under fire from eight ships. In the forty minutes before she can reply she loses her mizzen-topmast, her wheel and some fifty men. A double-headed shot kills eight marines on the poop; another passes between Nelson and Hardy.

  9. 12.45 pm

    Victory passes close under Bucentaure's stern and rakes her, dismounting twenty guns and by French account striking down some four hundred men. She then runs aboard Redoutable, whose captain, Lucas, has trained his crew in musketry and grenades for exactly this.

  10. 1.15 pm

    A ball from Redoutable's mizzen-top, fifty feet away, enters Nelson's left shoulder, passes through his lung and lodges in his spine. “They have done for me at last, Hardy. My backbone is shot through.” He is carried to the cockpit with a handkerchief over his face and decorations so the men will not know.

  11. 1.30 pm

    Redoutable's boarders mass to cross to Victory's swept upper deck. Téméraire comes up on her other side and fires a broadside that cuts down two hundred of them.

  12. 1.55 pm

    Redoutable strikes: of 643 men, 300 are dead and 222 badly wounded. Fougueux, drifting on to Téméraire, is boarded and taken in ten minutes.

  13. 2.05 pm

    Bucentaure, dismasted, strikes. Villeneuve surrenders to Captain James Atcherley of Conqueror's marines.

  14. 2.15 pm

    Santa Ana strikes to Royal Sovereign. Santísima Trinidad lies dismasted and silent.

  15. 2.30 pm

    Hardy comes below. “Well, Hardy, how goes the battle?” “Very well, my Lord. We have got twelve or fourteen of the enemy's ships in our possession.” Above them, Dumanoir is at last bringing the allied van round.

  16. 3.30 pm

    Hardy's second visit: fourteen or fifteen taken. “That is well, but I bargained for twenty. Anchor, Hardy, anchor!” Then: “Kiss me, Hardy.” Hardy kneels and kisses his cheek, and then his forehead.

  17. 4.30 pm

    Nelson dies, three hours and a quarter after he was hit. His last distinct words, repeated several times, are “Thank God, I have done my duty.”

  18. 5.00 pm

    Intrépide strikes, then Neptuno. Gravina, his arm shattered, leads eleven ships away to Cádiz.

  19. 5.45 pm

    The French Achille blows up. The battle is over.

The storm, and what was left

Nelson's dying order to anchor was sound. A heavy swell had been rolling in from the west all day, and that night it began to blow. The gale lasted the best part of a week and did more damage than the guns. Collingwood, with a fleet of cripples on a lee shore and thousands of prisoners, did not anchor at once, and later ordered the worst of the prizes scuttled or burnt.

On 23 October Commodore Cosmao-Kerjulien came out of Cádiz with five ships and retook Santa Ana and Neptuno, at the cost of three more of his own wrecked. Of the seventeen prizes only four reached Gibraltar: the French Swiftsure and the Spanish Bahama, San Ildefonso and San Juan Nepomuceno. On 4 November Sir Richard Strachan caught Dumanoir's four fugitives off Cape Ortegal and took them all.

British and Spanish sailors spent the storm saving each other from sinking ships. The people of Cádiz nursed British wounded washed ashore; Collingwood sent the Spanish wounded in under flag of truce, and the governor sent back casks of wine.

What it decided

Trafalgar did not stop the invasion of England. Napoleon had broken up the camp at Boulogne two months earlier, and on the day before the battle had taken an Austrian army at Ulm. What Trafalgar settled was the longer question. France and Spain never again offered a fleet action. For the remaining ten years of the war, and in practice for a century after it, Britain's command of the sea was not seriously disputed.

Lieutenant Lapenotière brought the dispatch home in the schooner Pickle, reaching the Admiralty at one in the morning of 6 November. “Sir, we have gained a great victory,” he told the Secretary, “but we have lost Lord Nelson.”

Victory's own loss

57 killed and 102 wounded out of 821, more than any other British ship but Colossus. Her mizzen-mast was gone, the others were tottering, and she had to be towed to Gibraltar by Neptune.

Things worth knowing

The signal nearly read differently

Nelson first asked for “Nelson confides that every man will do his duty”. An officer suggested England for Nelson; Pasco suggested expects. Collingwood's reaction on seeing flags going up yet again was, “I wish Nelson would stop signalling. We all know what we have to do.” The flags are shown here.

He was asked to cover his stars

Nelson wore his ordinary undress coat with the four embroidered orders sewn on its breast, as he did every day. Several officers wanted to ask him to change or cover it; none quite dared, and by the time Hardy raised it Nelson said it was too late to be shifting a coat.

The man who shot him

Nobody knows who fired. Redoutable's mizzen-top was cleared soon afterwards by two of Victory's midshipmen, John Pollard and Francis Collingwood, firing from the poop. A Frenchman named Robert Guillemard later published a memoir claiming the shot; it is a known fabrication.

Women and children

Some British ships had women aboard, against regulations, who carried powder and helped the surgeons. When the French Achille burned, Pickle's boats picked up a naked woman clinging to wreckage: Jeannette, wife of one of the topmen, who was found alive in another ship days later.

The brandy

Nelson had asked not to be thrown overboard. Surgeon Beatty put the body in a leaguer, the largest size of cask, filled with brandy, lashed to the mainmast and under sentry. At Gibraltar the brandy was topped up with spirits of wine. The story that the crew drank it is a later invention, though it gave the Navy the phrase “tapping the Admiral”.
