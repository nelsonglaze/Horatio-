After 1805

Legacy & controversies

Britain buried Nelson as a saint, and for a century nobody was encouraged to look closely at the parts of his life that did not fit. Here is what was built in his memory, what is commonly believed about him and is not true, and what is true and not to his credit.

The funeral

Victory reached the Thames estuary just before Christmas 1805. The body was taken out of its cask, found in good condition, and placed in the coffin Captain Hallowell had made from the mast of L'Orient, which went inside a lead coffin, inside a gilded outer one.

For three days in January it lay in state in the Painted Hall at Greenwich Hospital. Thousands filed past each day and many more were turned away. On 8 January it was rowed upriver in the old state barge of Charles II, at the head of a procession of boats two miles long, to Whitehall Stairs, and rested overnight at the Admiralty.

The land procession next day was so long that its head had reached St Paul's before its tail left the Admiralty: thirty-two admirals, more than a hundred captains, some ten thousand troops, and forty-eight seamen and marines from Victory carrying her shot-torn colours. The Prince of Wales and his brothers followed. The service lasted four hours and ended by torchlight. As the coffin went down through the floor, the Victory's men were meant to fold the ship's ensign and lay it on top. Instead they tore the largest of the flags to pieces among themselves and put the scraps inside their jackets.

He lies in the crypt directly under the centre of the dome, in a black marble sarcophagus carved in the 1520s for Cardinal Wolsey, confiscated by Henry VIII and left unused at Windsor for nearly three hundred years.

Neither of the women in his life was there. Women did not attend state funerals; Fanny stayed at home in mourning, and Emma, who had no standing at all, was said to have been unable to leave her bed.

What the country gave

An earldom, £5,000 a year and £99,000 for an estate to his brother William; £15,000 to each sister; £2,000 a year to his widow. Nothing to Emma Hamilton or to Horatia, in spite of the last request he made of his country.

Stone, bronze and ceremony

The first monuments went up within months: an obelisk on Glasgow Green in 1806, a pillar in Dublin, a column in Montreal and a statue in Birmingham in 1809, the tower on Calton Hill in Edinburgh, the Britannia Monument on the dunes at Great Yarmouth. London was slow. Trafalgar Square was laid out in the 1830s, and the column in its centre was finished in 1843: about 169 feet high, with a seventeen-foot statue by E. H. Baily facing south down Whitehall towards the Admiralty and the sea. The four bronze reliefs at its foot show St Vincent, the Nile, Copenhagen and his death, and the lions were added in 1867.

The Navy keeps 21 October as Trafalgar Day. Officers dine that night and drink one toast in silence: “The Immortal Memory.” Victory flies the signal. His plan of attack entered the service's idea of itself so thoroughly that a hundred years later admirals were being judged by whether they had shown “the Nelson touch”, and Jellicoe, who fought a cautious and sensible battle at Jutland in 1916, never quite escaped the comparison.

The influence went abroad. Before the battle of Tsushima in 1905 Admiral Tōgō, who had trained in England, hoisted a signal modelled on Nelson's: the fate of the Empire depends on this battle; let every man do his utmost.

Not every monument has lasted. Nelson's Pillar in Dublin was blown up by republicans in 1966. The statue in Bridgetown, Barbados, put up in 1813, was taken down in 2020.

Things people believe

  • He wore an eye patch. Never. The blind eye looked normal. He had a green shade fitted to his hat brim to protect the good one. The patch arrived with Victorian illustrators and stuck.
  • “I see no ships.” Not his words. At Copenhagen he said, “I really do not see the signal.”
  • He was tiny. About five feet six, a normal height for the time. He was thin.
  • He was pickled in rum, and the sailors drank it. It was brandy, then spirits of wine, under guard. Nobody drank it. Navy rum has been called “Nelson's blood” ever since anyway.
  • Sailors' uniforms mourn him. The black silk and the three white stripes on a seaman's collar are said to mark his death and his three victories. Both are older or later than him and have nothing to do with either.
  • He said “Kismet, Hardy”. He said “Kiss me”, and Hardy did.

Naples, 1799

The part of his record that his admirers have always found hardest.

After the Nile, Nelson based himself at Naples, the capital of a Bourbon kingdom ruled in practice by its queen, Maria Carolina, sister of Marie Antoinette. He was ill, exhausted, flattered by the court and falling in love with the British envoy's wife, who was the queen's closest friend. He urged King Ferdinand to march on the French in Rome. The Neapolitan army ran, the French advanced, and in December 1798 Nelson carried the royal family and their treasure to Sicily. Behind them, with French support, liberal nobles and professional men proclaimed a republic.

In June 1799 a peasant army raised by Cardinal Ruffo in the king's name retook the city with great savagery. The republicans held out in two sea-forts. To stop the bloodshed Ruffo offered them terms: they could march out with the honours of war and either go home unmolested or be shipped to France. The treaty was signed by Ruffo, by the Russian and Turkish commanders, and by Captain Edward Foote of the Royal Navy.

Nelson arrived with his fleet the next day, 24 June. He declared that a cardinal had no power to treat with rebels and that the capitulation could not be carried out without the king's approval. After two days of argument a message went ashore that he would not oppose the embarkation. The republicans came out of the forts and went aboard the transports that were to take them to Toulon. The transports were then moved under the guns of the British ships and held. When the king arrived, the prisoners were handed over to his courts. About a hundred were executed, among them some of the most distinguished scholars, lawyers and physicians in southern Italy, and many more were imprisoned or exiled.

Whether Nelson deliberately tricked the garrisons into coming out, or allowed them to deceive themselves, has been argued for two centuries. What is not in doubt is that men surrendered on the strength of a treaty bearing a British officer's signature and were hanged.

Caracciolo

Commodore Francesco Caracciolo was the senior officer of the Neapolitan navy, a prince, forty-seven years old, who had followed the king to Sicily, gone back with permission to save his estates, and then taken command of the republic's gunboats and fired on a royal frigate. He was caught in disguise on 29 June and brought aboard Nelson's flagship in irons.

Nelson ordered a court martial of Neapolitan officers to sit at once in Foudroyant's wardroom. It began at noon and found him guilty by a majority. Nelson directed that he be hanged at five o'clock the same afternoon from the yardarm of the frigate he had fired on, the body to hang until sunset and then be cut down into the sea. Requests for a second trial, for a day to prepare himself, and to be shot as an officer instead of hanged were all refused.

Nelson had some legal authority for it, as the king's representative, and Caracciolo had certainly borne arms against his sovereign. The haste was Nelson's own. Charles James Fox raised it in the House of Commons within months. Robert Southey, in the biography that made Nelson a national hero, called the whole episode “a deplorable transaction, a stain upon the memory of Nelson and the honour of England”, and added that to palliate it would be in vain.

That summer he also ignored repeated orders from his commander-in-chief, Lord Keith, to send ships to defend Minorca, because he would not leave Naples. The Admiralty censured him. He was created Duke of Bronté by the grateful king.

How historians divide

Nineteenth-century biographers either passed over Naples or blamed Emma Hamilton and the queen. A. T. Mahan argued in the 1890s that Nelson acted within his powers. Recent biographers, working from the Neapolitan as well as the British papers, mostly conclude that he had the authority he claimed and used it with a harshness that was out of character and that he never afterwards admitted.

Captain Foote

The officer who had signed the treaty protested at the time and, when Nelson's defenders later blamed him for it, published his own Vindication in 1807.

Other charges

His wife

Fanny Nelson did nothing to deserve what happened to her. She had waited through seven years of war, nursed him after Tenerife and looked after his father. He left her within weeks of coming home, publicly, for a woman whose husband was living in the same house, and then refused to read her letters. He was generous with money and cold in everything else. His family, who had been fond of her, dropped her to keep in with Emma. It is the plainest case in his life of his treating someone badly because it suited him. The story is on the life page.

Slavery

Nelson spent years on the West India station, married into a planter family on Nevis, and counted Jamaica planters among his friends. In June 1805, from Victory, he wrote privately to one of them, Simon Taylor, that he had been “bred in the good old school” to value the West India colonies, and that their rights would not be infringed “whilst I have an arm to fight in their defence, or a tongue to launch my voice against the damnable and cursed doctrine of Wilberforce and his hypocritical allies”.

He never spoke on the subject in the House of Lords, and the letter was unknown until opponents of abolition printed it, with alterations, after his death. His reasons, as far as they can be read, were strategic and conventional: he thought the colonies vital to British sea power and abolition a danger to them. But the words are his. Since 2017 they have been at the centre of an argument in Britain and the Caribbean about how he should be commemorated.

Vanity and disobedience

He wanted titles, ribbons and applause, said so, and wore every star he was entitled to. He wrote up his own exploits and sent them to the newspapers. He stretched his orders under Jervis at St Vincent, flatly disobeyed Keith in 1799 and ignored Parker's signal at Copenhagen. Two of those three times he was right, which is what made him dangerous as an example. Lord St Vincent, who made his career, said that his sole merit was animal courage and that his private character was disgraceful in every sense. St Vincent also said there was but one Nelson.

The Copenhagen letter

The message that ended the battle of Copenhagen threatened to burn captured Danish batteries with their crews aboard. Nelson always maintained it was written from humanity. It also got his ships out of a very bad position. In Denmark it is generally regarded as a bluff that worked.

“He is in many points a really great man, in others a baby.”
Lord Minto, who knew him well, 1802
The balance

The same man prayed before Trafalgar that humanity after victory should be the mark of the British fleet, sent boats to save his enemies from the burning Orient, and refused a retrial to a prisoner who had four hours to live. A site about him ought to be able to say all three.
