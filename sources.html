Sources, caveats & credits

Where this comes from

Nelson is one of the best-documented people of his century. That does not mean the documents agree.

How far to trust it

The battle plans

The animated plans are reconstructions built from the standard narratives. They are meant to show how each action unfolded and why it went as it did. They are not surveys. Ships are drawn several times too large so that they can be seen; positions are approximate; and where ships milled about in smoke for hours, as at Trafalgar and Cape St Vincent, the plan shows a plausible version of a mêlée nobody could map at the time.

Times are a particular problem. Each ship kept her own log by her own clock, and at Trafalgar logs differ by as much as an hour about the same event. The times given here are the ones most often used.

The figures

British casualties come from the returns printed with the commanders' dispatches in the London Gazette and are reliable to within a few men; later compilations sometimes add those who died of wounds. Enemy casualties are estimates, and the ranges on the reckoning page reflect real disagreement between sources. Gun counts are the nominal rate of each ship; most carried carronades in addition that were not counted.

The words

Letters and the Trafalgar memorandum are quoted from Nicolas's edition of the dispatches. Things Nelson is reported to have said aloud come from people who were there, written down days or years afterwards: Colonel Stewart for Copenhagen, Beatty, Scott and Burke for his death. A few sayings rest on tradition only, and the text says so where that is the case.

If you find a mistake, it is the site's and not the sources'.

Books

  • Sir Nicholas Harris Nicolas, ed., The Dispatches and Letters of Vice Admiral Lord Viscount Nelson, 7 volumes, 1844–46. The foundation of everything since.
  • William Beatty, Authentic Narrative of the Death of Lord Nelson, 1807. By Victory's surgeon.
  • James Stanier Clarke and John M'Arthur, The Life of Admiral Lord Nelson, 1809. The official life, with his own “Sketch of my Life”.
  • Robert Southey, The Life of Nelson, 1813. The book that fixed the legend, and still readable.
  • William James, The Naval History of Great Britain, 1822–24. Ship-by-ship accounts and casualty lists for every action.
  • A. T. Mahan, The Life of Nelson: The Embodiment of the Sea Power of Great Britain, 1897.
  • Julian S. Corbett, The Campaign of Trafalgar, 1910.
  • Carola Oman, Nelson, 1947.
  • Brian Lavery, Nelson and the Nile, 1998.
  • Colin White, 1797: Nelson's Year of Destiny, 1998.
  • Ole Feldbæk, The Battle of Copenhagen 1801, English edition 2002. The Danish side.
  • Roy Adkins, Trafalgar: The Biography of a Battle, 2004.
  • Andrew Lambert, Nelson: Britannia's God of War, 2004.
  • John Sugden, Nelson: A Dream of Glory, 2004, and Nelson: The Sword of Albion, 2012. The fullest modern life.
  • Roger Knight, The Pursuit of Victory: The Life and Achievement of Horatio Nelson, 2005.
  • N. A. M. Rodger, The Command of the Ocean: A Naval History of Britain 1649–1815, 2004. For the Navy he served in.

Places

  • National Museum of the Royal Navy, Portsmouth: HMS Victory, the Trafalgar sail and the funeral barge.
  • Royal Museums Greenwich: the coat he was wearing when he was shot, with the hole in the left shoulder, and most of the paintings reproduced here.
  • St Paul's Cathedral, London: the tomb, in the crypt.
  • All Saints' Church, Burnham Thorpe, Norfolk, where his father was rector.
HMS Victory and The Big Repair

Details of the conservation project come from the National Museum of the Royal Navy's announcements. Costs and dates will change as the work goes on; the museum's project hub is the place to check. The donation links are on the Victory page.

Spelling

Ship names follow the navy they belonged to: Santísima Trinidad, Guillaume Tell, Prøvesteenen. Where a prize was renamed, both names are given.
