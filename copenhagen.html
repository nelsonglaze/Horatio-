Battles  ·  2 April 1801

The Battle of Copenhagen

Nelson takes twelve ships of the line up a narrow channel against a moored line of Danish hulks, floating batteries and forts. Three run aground before a shot is fired. Ordered to break off, he puts his telescope to his blind eye, and four hours later the Danes agree to a truce.

A quarrel over timber and trade

Copenhagen was fought against a country with which Britain was not quite at war. In December 1800 Tsar Paul of Russia, an admirer of Bonaparte, revived the Armed Neutrality of the North: Russia, Denmark, Sweden and Prussia would resist the British practice of stopping and searching neutral ships for French goods. The Royal Navy was built of Baltic timber, rigged with Baltic hemp and caulked with Baltic tar. The government decided to break the league before the ice melted and its three fleets could combine.

Command went to Admiral Sir Hyde Parker, sixty-one, cautious and recently married to a girl of eighteen. Nelson was his second. The fleet sailed from Yarmouth on 12 March, only after Nelson had prodded the Admiralty into prodding Parker. A diplomatic mission to Copenhagen failed. On 30 March the fleet ran the Sound under the guns of Kronborg Castle, keeping to the Swedish side, where not a gun fired.

At the council of war Parker dwelt on the difficulties. Nelson paced the cabin. “The more numerous the better,” he said of the Swedes, and of the Russians, “So much the better; I wish they were twice as many.” He offered to attack with ten ships of the line. Parker gave him twelve, and every frigate and small craft in the fleet.

The problem

The Danes had spent the weeks of British delay well. Their line could not be attacked from the north without passing the Trekroner fort, sixty-eight heavy guns on piles at the harbour mouth. Nelson chose to come at it from the other end. That meant taking his division down the Outer Deep, round the tail of a long shoal called the Middle Ground, and back up the King's Deep, a channel three-quarters of a mile wide that the Danes had stripped of every buoy and marker.

For two nights Nelson and Captain Riou sounded and re-buoyed the Outer Deep from open boats in drifting ice. On 1 April the division sailed down it and anchored two miles from the southern end of the Danish line. That night Captain Hardy had himself rowed with muffled oars right round the leading Danish ship, feeling for the bottom with a pole so that no splash of a lead-line would be heard. Nelson dictated orders from his cot until one in the morning; six clerks copied them until dawn.

“It is warm work; and this day may be the last to any of us at a moment. But mark you! I would not be elsewhere for thousands.”
Nelson to Colonel Stewart on Elephant's quarterdeck, as a shot struck the mainmast

Signal No. 39

Parker lay four miles off with eight ships, unable to get nearer against wind and current. What he could see through the smoke was three British 74s aground, flying signals of distress, and the Danish fire as heavy after three hours as at the start. He decided to give Nelson a way out: if he was in a state to continue he would disregard the signal, and if not, it would be his excuse. At about a quarter past one, London hoisted No. 39, discontinue the engagement.

Nelson's signal lieutenant reported it and asked whether to repeat it. “No, acknowledge it,” said Nelson, and asked if his own signal for close action was still flying. Told that it was: “Mind you keep it so.” He walked the deck working the stump of his right arm, as he did when angry. “Leave off action! Now damn me if I do.” Then, to his flag-captain: “You know, Foley, I have only one eye. I have a right to be blind sometimes.” He put the glass to his right eye. “I really do not see the signal.”

Admiral Graves, in Defiance, repeated No. 39 where Parker could see it and kept No. 16 flying where Nelson could. No ship of the line moved. Riou's frigates, nearest the flagship and under the guns of Trekroner, had no such choice.

The letter

By two o'clock most of the Danish line had stopped firing, but the battle would not end. Hulks that had struck were re-manned by boat from the shore and opened fire again; British boats sent to take possession were shot at; the Trekroner batteries fired over the prizes into the British ships. Nelson wrote a note at the rudder-head, and sent below for a candle and wax when his secretary offered a wafer. A hurried seal, he said, would tell the Danes he was in a hurry.

“To the Brothers of Englishmen, the Danes. Lord Nelson has directions to spare Denmark when no longer resisting; but if the firing is continued on the part of Denmark, Lord Nelson will be obliged to set on fire all the floating batteries he has taken, without having the power of saving the brave Danes who have defended them.”

The Crown Prince, watching from the quay, sent to ask what the message meant, and then ordered a cease-fire. Whether the letter was an act of humanity or a threat that got a battered squadron out from under the forts has been argued ever since. Nelson said the first. Several of his own officers thought the second; it was certainly both. Elephant and Defiance ran aground on the way out.

The pilots

The fleet's pilots were mates of Baltic merchantmen, used to bringing ships of 300 tons through the Sound in peace. Asked on the morning of the battle who would lead a 74 up the King's Deep, they would not. Alexander Briarly, master of Bellona, volunteered and went aboard Edgar.

A boy of seventeen

Sub-Lieutenant Peter Willemoes fought a raft of twenty-four guns for four hours directly under Elephant's broadside. Nelson told the Crown Prince he ought to be made an admiral. “If I made all my brave officers admirals,” said the Prince, “I should have no captains or lieutenants left.”

The day, hour by hour

  1. 8.00 am

    Captains called aboard Elephant for their orders. The wind has come round to south-south-east overnight, fair for the King's Deep.

  2. 9.30 am

    Signal to weigh in succession. Edgar leads.

  3. 10.05 am

    Prøvesteenen opens fire on Edgar. Agamemnon, anchored too far to leeward, cannot clear the shoal and goes aground.

  4. 10.45 am

    Bellona and Russell take the ground on the Middle Ground. Nelson, coming up next, puts his helm the other way and passes inside them. He re-allots stations by signal as he goes.

  5. 11.30 am

    Nine ships of the line anchored and in close action. Dannebrog is on fire; Commodore Fischer shifts his flag to Holsteen.

  6. 1.00 pm

    Monarch and Defiance, at the head of the line, are under fire from the Trekroner fort as well as their opponents. Isis and Bellona have had guns burst.

  7. 1.15 pm

    Parker makes signal No. 39. Nelson does not see it.

  8. 1.30 pm

    Riou hauls off his frigates. “What will Nelson think of us?” A raking shot from Trekroner kills him as Amazon turns.

  9. 2.00 pm

    Fire from the southern half of the Danish line has almost ceased. Fischer shifts his flag again, to the Trekroner.

  10. 2.30 pm

    Nelson sends his letter ashore with Captain Sir Frederick Thesiger under a flag of truce.

  11. 3.00 pm

    The Crown Prince's adjutant, Lindholm, comes off with a flag of truce. Firing stops.

  12. 4.30 pm

    Dannebrog, adrift and burning since she struck, blows up.

After the firing stopped

Nelson went ashore next day through a crowd that was curious and, by his account, not hostile, and dined with the Crown Prince. He negotiated in person for a week, at one point remarking loudly in English that he could bombard the city that night, and on 9 April got an armistice of fourteen weeks: long enough, he reckoned, to deal with the Russians.

It turned out not to be needed. Tsar Paul had been strangled by his own officers on the night of 23 March, nine days before the battle. The news reached Copenhagen during the talks. His son Alexander wanted peace, and the Armed Neutrality came apart. Parker was recalled in May and Nelson given the Baltic command; he took the fleet to Reval to find the Russians friendly and the season's work done.

He was made a viscount. But because Denmark had not been formally an enemy, the City of London voted no thanks and the government struck no medal, and the captains who had fought what Nelson called the hardest battle of his life got nothing. He never forgave it, and refused to dine with the Lord Mayor until the Copenhagen men were recognised.

Of the twelve prizes, eleven were burnt where they lay. Only Holsteen was thought worth the trouble of sailing home. The Danes had lost old hulks they had never meant to send to sea; their real fleet lay untouched in the dockyard, where the British came back for it in 1807.

“I have been in a hundred and five engagements, but that of today is the most terrible of them all.”
Nelson, on the evening of 2 April 1801
Why the Danes were so hard to beat

Their guns were on steady platforms and their ships did not need to be sailed, so every man could fight. When a crew was shot down, another came out from the city by boat. Students, clerks and craftsmen served guns beside the seamen.

Bligh

William Bligh, of the Bounty mutiny twelve years before, commanded Glatton next in line to Elephant. Nelson called him aboard afterwards to thank him, and Bligh asked for, and got, a written testimonial.
