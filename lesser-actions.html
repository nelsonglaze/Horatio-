Battles  ·  1780–1805

The lesser actions

Between the four great days lie twenty-five years of sieges, boat fights and single-ship actions. They cost Nelson an eye and an arm, taught him most of what he knew, and include his only two defeats. Three of them have animated plans: the Ça Ira, Tenerife and the long chase of 1805.

Fort San Juan, Nicaragua

March to April 1780  ·  Captain, HMS Hinchinbrook, aged 21

The governor of Jamaica had a scheme to cut Spain's American empire in half by seizing the San Juan river and Lake Nicaragua. Nelson's part was to convoy the troops to the river mouth. He volunteered to go further, and took some two hundred seamen and marines up a hundred miles of unmapped river in ships' boats and canoes, hauling them through shallows in tropical rain.

He led the rush that took the outpost on San Bartolomé island, sited the batteries against the Castillo de la Inmaculada Concepción, and laid most of the guns himself. The fort surrendered on 29 April. By then dysentery and fever were destroying the expedition, and Nelson had already been carried down-river in a canoe to take up a new command he was too ill to hold.

The force lost far more men to disease than to the Spanish. Of Hinchinbrook's complement of two hundred, Nelson believed not more than ten survived. He was nursed at Port Royal by Cuba Cornwallis, a freed woman famous on the station for bringing fever patients through, and sent home in the Lion a wreck.

“I want words to express the obligations I owe that gentleman. He was the first on every service, whether by night or by day.”
Colonel John Polson's dispatch
Result
Fort taken; expedition abandoned
Cost
Most of the force dead of fever
To Nelson
Dysentery and “Yellow Jack”; a year's convalescence

Bastia and Calvi, Corsica

April to August 1794  ·  Captain, HMS Agamemnon, aged 35

When the Royal Navy lost Toulon at the end of 1793 it needed another base in the western Mediterranean, and Lord Hood chose Corsica. The army's generals thought Bastia could not be taken with the troops available. Hood and Nelson took it without them. Nelson landed with Agamemnon's seamen, dragged 24-pounders up slopes the engineers had called impossible, and with about 1,200 men besieged a garrison of some 4,500, which surrendered on 22 May after six weeks.

At Calvi, with the army this time under the energetic Charles Stuart, he did the same work in the full heat of a Corsican summer. On the morning of 12 July a French shot struck the sandbags of the battery where he stood and drove sand and splintered stone into his face and chest. “I got a little hurt this morning,” he reported to Hood, “not much, as you may judge by my writing.” The right eye never again told more than light from dark. Calvi fell on 10 August, by which time half his men were down with malaria.

The eye was not disfigured and he never wore a patch over it. What appears in some portraits is a green shade, like the peak of a cap, which he had sewn under his hat to keep the glare from his good eye.

Result
Both towns taken; Corsica British until 1796
To Nelson
Sight of the right eye, 12 July 1794
A sea officer ashore

“We are few, but of the right sort,” Nelson wrote from the trenches. He never got over the army's caution in Corsica, and the experience fed a lifelong belief that a naval officer with seamen and guns could do anything.

Agamemnon and the Ça Ira

13–14 March 1795, off Genoa  ·  Captain, HMS Agamemnon

The French Toulon fleet put to sea to retake Corsica and met Admiral Hotham's fleet in the Gulf of Genoa. Neither admiral was eager. In the French line the 80-gun Ça Ira ran into her next ahead, lost her topmasts and dropped astern in tow of a frigate.

Agamemnon, the fastest ship in the British line, reached her first, far ahead of any support. The Frenchman was much the bigger ship and her stern guns could hurt, so Nelson did not range alongside. He ran up to within a hundred yards of her stern, put the helm over and fired his whole broadside into it; then turned back, caught up, and did it again. He kept this up for two and a half hours. Ça Ira lost 110 men that afternoon. Agamemnon had seven wounded.

Next morning Ça Ira and the 74 that had taken her in tow, Censeur, were cut off and taken. Nelson went aboard the flagship and urged Hotham to leave the prizes and chase the rest. “We must be contented,” said Hotham. “We have done very well.”

“Now, had we taken ten sail, and had allowed the eleventh to escape, when it had been possible to have got at her, I could never have called it well done.”
Nelson to his wife, 1 April 1795

The same thing happened off the Hyères Islands on 13 July: Agamemnon and a few others were in among the French rear, one ship had struck, and Hotham made the signal of recall.

Result
Ça Ira (80) and Censeur (74) taken
Lesson
A half-won battle is a battle lost

Minerve and the Santa Sabina

19–20 December 1796, off Cartagena  ·  Commodore, HMS La Minerve

On his way to evacuate Elba with two frigates, Nelson fell in with two Spanish frigates at night. Minerve fought the 40-gun Santa Sabina for nearly three hours before she struck, with 164 of her crew killed or wounded. Her captain turned out to be Don Jacobo Stuart, great-grandson of James II of England.

At dawn a Spanish squadron including two ships of the line came over the horizon. Lieutenants Culverhouse and Hardy, in the prize, hoisted British colours over Spanish to draw the pursuit on to themselves, and were retaken; Minerve, her masts wounded, got away. Nelson sent Stuart back to Cartagena under flag of truce with his sword, and asked for Hardy in exchange. He got him in time for Cape St Vincent.

Result
Santa Sabina taken, then lost again
Notable
The start of Nelson's reliance on Thomas Hardy

The boats off Cádiz

Night of 3 July 1797  ·  Rear-Admiral, HMS Theseus

After St Vincent the Spanish fleet lay blockaded in Cádiz, and Jervis gave Nelson the inshore squadron. To provoke the Spaniards out, Nelson brought a bomb vessel in to shell the town. The Spanish sent gunboats and armed launches against her, and Nelson went in with his own boats to meet them.

His barge, with ten oarsmen, Captain Fremantle and the coxswain John Sykes, was laid alongside the launch of the Spanish commander, Don Miguel Tyrason, which carried about thirty men. It was fought out with cutlasses across the gunwales. Eighteen of the Spaniards were killed and the rest wounded before the launch was taken. Twice Sykes parried cuts aimed at the admiral, and a third he could stop only by putting his own head in the way.

“It was during this period,” Nelson wrote in the sketch of his life, “that perhaps my personal courage was more conspicuous than at any other part of my life.”

Santa Cruz de Tenerife

22–25 July 1797  ·  Rear-Admiral, HMS Theseus, aged 38

Word came that a treasure ship from Manila had put into Santa Cruz in the Canaries. Jervis gave Nelson three 74s, a 50, three frigates and a cutter to fetch her out. The first plan, to land by surprise and seize the heights above the town, was beaten by currents and a calm on the 22nd, and the alarm was given.

Nelson should have left. Instead, on the night of the 24th, he took about a thousand men in boats straight at the town mole. General Antonio Gutiérrez had guns and militia waiting. The surf and the dark scattered the boats; grapeshot swept the mole; the cutter Fox was hit below the waterline and went down with ninety-seven men.

Nelson was stepping ashore, sword drawn, when a ball smashed his right arm above the elbow. His stepson Josiah Nisbet got a tourniquet on it and the boat off the beach. The nearest ship was Seahorse; Nelson refused to be taken aboard, because Captain Fremantle's young wife was in her and her husband was still ashore. At Theseus he would not be hoisted in. “Let me alone. I have yet my legs left and one arm. Tell the surgeon to make haste and get his instruments. I know I must lose my right arm, so the sooner it is off the better.” It was taken off high up within the half-hour. What he remembered afterwards was the coldness of the knife, and he ordered that surgeons in his ships should keep hot water by to warm their instruments.

Ashore, Troubridge with some 340 men had reached the town square and was surrounded. He sent to say he would burn the town unless given terms. Gutiérrez, who knew it was a bluff, gave them anyway: the British would re-embark with their arms, in Spanish boats if need be, and attack the islands no more. He fed them, took their wounded into his hospital, and sent wine. Nelson sent back cheese and a cask of English beer, and carried the governor's dispatch announcing the British defeat to Cádiz for him.

“I am become a burthen to my friends and useless to my Country. A left-handed Admiral will never again be considered as useful.”
His first letter written with the left hand, to Jervis, 27 July 1797
Result
Defeat; force re-embarked on terms
British loss
About 250 killed, drowned or wounded
To Nelson
Right arm amputated
Jervis's reply

“Mortals cannot command success; you and your companions have certainly deserved it.” Santa Cruz still marks 25 July, and flags taken from the British hang in its museum.

The Généreux

18 February 1800, off Malta  ·  Rear-Admiral, HMS Foudroyant

Two French ships of the line had escaped from Aboukir Bay, and Nelson wanted them. In February 1800 a relief convoy for the French garrison of Malta, escorted by the Généreux, ran into his squadron. The frigate Success crossed her bows and raked her, killing Rear-Admiral Perrée; Foudroyant and Northumberland came up, and she struck after a single broadside.

A midshipman on Foudroyant's quarterdeck ducked as the first shot came over. Nelson patted his head and told him that Charles XII of Sweden had run away from the first shot he heard, and been called “the Great” afterwards for his bravery. “I therefore hope much from you in future.”

The other fugitive, Guillaume Tell, was taken by Foudroyant and two consorts on 30 March. Nelson was not aboard. He was at Palermo with the Hamiltons, against the wishes of his commander-in-chief, which is part of the story told on the legacy page.

Result
Généreux (74) taken
Significance
With Guillaume Tell, completed the destruction of Brueys's fleet

Boulogne

Night of 15–16 August 1801  ·  Vice-Admiral, HMS Medusa

Back from the Baltic, Nelson was given a command meant mostly to calm the public: the frigates, gun-brigs and fencibles guarding the coast from Sussex to Suffolk against Bonaparte's invasion barges. He decided to cut the flotilla out of Boulogne roads.

Fifty-seven boats went in at night in four divisions. The tide swept them apart, so that they arrived one division at a time. Rear-Admiral Latouche-Tréville had been warned by an earlier bombardment: his vessels were moored with chains instead of cables, hung with boarding nets and full of soldiers. The boats that got alongside could neither board nor tow anything away. Forty-four men were killed and 128 wounded for nothing.

Among the dying was Commander Edward Parker, a young officer Nelson had brought on and treated as a son. “He was my child,” he wrote, “for I found him in distress.” He paid for the funeral and wept at it.

Result
Repulsed; no vessel taken
British loss
44 killed, 128 wounded
The same opponent

Latouche-Tréville later commanded at Toulon during Nelson's blockade, and boasted in print that Nelson had run from him. Nelson kept the cutting. Latouche died in 1804 before they could meet.

Toulon and the long chase

July 1803 to August 1805  ·  Vice-Admiral, HMS Victory

When war began again in 1803 Nelson was given the Mediterranean. For two years he watched the French fleet in Toulon, not by sitting off the port but by hanging back out of sight with frigates on watch, hoping it would come out. His anchorage, when he needed one, was an inlet he had found among the Maddalena islands off Sardinia. He bought bullocks, onions and lemons wherever they could be had, kept the men busy and amused, and had almost no sick list in a fleet that hardly saw a dockyard. He himself did not set foot out of Victory from June 1803 to July 1805.

On 30 March 1805 Villeneuve got out. Nelson, guessing Egypt again, covered the wrong direction, then fought headwinds for a month to reach Gibraltar. On 11 May, sure at last that the French had gone to the West Indies, he followed with ten ships against eighteen. He reached Barbados on 4 June, having gained ten days on them. False information from a British general sent him south to Trinidad while Villeneuve was to the north; but the news that Nelson was in the Caribbean was enough, and Villeneuve started back for Europe at once.

Nelson guessed that too, sent the brig Curieux ahead with a warning, and recrossed the Atlantic. The brig sighted the Combined Fleet on the way, and her report put Calder's squadron in its path off Finisterre. On 20 July Nelson landed at Gibraltar, writing in his diary that he had gone ashore “for the first time since June 16th, 1803; and from having my foot out of the Victory, two years wanting ten days”.

He had sailed some seven thousand miles and not fired a gun. But the West Indies trade was safe and Napoleon's plan for the Channel was in pieces. Three months later Villeneuve came out of Cádiz to Trafalgar.

Odds
10 ships of the line chasing 18
Distance
About 7,000 nautical miles, out and back
Result
Villeneuve driven from the Caribbean within days of Nelson's arrival
