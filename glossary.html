Sea terms

A glossary

The language of a sailing navy was exact because it had to be: an order misunderstood in a gale could lose the ship. These are the words used on this site, grouped by what they are about.

Wind and direction

Windward, weather
The side or direction the wind is blowing from. The weather side of a ship is the side the wind strikes.
Leeward, lee
The side or direction the wind is blowing towards; downwind. Said “loo'ard”. A ship's lee side is sheltered and, when she heels, the lower one.
Weather gage
The position to windward of an enemy. The fleet that held it could choose when to attack, since it could run down on the enemy at will, while the fleet to leeward had to beat slowly upwind to close.
Lee shore
A coast lying downwind of a ship, on to which the wind is trying to drive her. The most feared situation at sea, and the reason Nelson's last order was to anchor.
Starboard, larboard
The right-hand and left-hand sides of a ship looking forward. Larboard was the word in Nelson's day; port replaced it officially in 1844 because the two were too easily confused when shouted.
Close-hauled
Sailing as nearly into the wind as the ship can manage, about six points or 67 degrees off it for a square-rigged ship. Also “on a wind” or “by the wind”.
Large, before the wind
With the wind well aft. Sailing large means with the wind on the quarter; before the wind, dead astern.
Point
One thirty-second of the compass circle: 11¼ degrees.
Van, centre, rear
The leading, middle and last divisions of a fleet in line.
Ahead, abeam, astern
In front; directly out to the side; behind. The quarter is the direction halfway between abeam and astern, and the bow halfway between ahead and abeam.

Handling the ship

Tack
To turn the ship's bows through the wind so that it comes on the other side. Quick, and gains ground to windward, but can fail in light airs or with damaged rigging. Also the side the wind is on: a ship with the wind on her right side is on the starboard tack.
Wear
To turn the ship the other way, stern through the wind, in a wide circle downwind. Slower and loses ground to leeward, but it is sure. Nelson wore out of the line at Cape St Vincent.
In succession, together
A line tacking in succession follows its leader round, each ship turning at the same spot. Tacking together, every ship turns at once and the order of the line is reversed.
Bear up, bear away
To turn away from the wind. Its opposite is to haul up, luff or haul one's wind: to turn towards it.
Back a sail, heave to
To swing a sail so the wind presses on its front and stops the ship. A ship hove to has some sails backed and some drawing, and lies nearly still.
Spring
A second rope led from an anchored ship's stern to her anchor cable. Hauling on it swings the ship so her broadside can be pointed. The French at the Nile had not rigged them.
Anchor by the stern
To drop an anchor from the stern instead of the bow, so that the ship stops without swinging round. Done at the Nile and Copenhagen to bring up beam-on to an enemy at once.
Aground
Stuck on the bottom. Culloden at the Nile; Agamemnon, Bellona and Russell at Copenhagen.
Jury rig
A makeshift mast or rudder set up at sea after damage.

Fighting

Broadside
All the guns on one side of a ship, or their firing together. A ship could fire almost nothing directly ahead or astern.
Line of battle, line ahead
A fleet in single file, each ship following the next, so that every broadside bears and every bow and stern is covered. The standard fighting formation from the 1650s to Trafalgar.
Rake
To fire along the length of an enemy ship through her bow or, better, her stern. The shot travels the whole gun deck. The most destructive thing one ship could do to another.
Break the line
To sail through a gap in the enemy's line, raking the ships on either side and cutting his fleet in two.
Double
To attack a part of the enemy's line from both sides at once, as at the Nile.
Board
To put men on to an enemy ship and take her hand to hand. A ship that runs against another is said to run aboard or foul of her.
Strike
To haul down the ship's colours as a sign of surrender. A ship that has struck must stop firing and must not be fired on.
Prize
A captured ship. Her value was shared out among the captors as prize money, by fixed proportions that made admirals rich and gave a seaman a few pounds.
Cutting out
Seizing an enemy vessel at anchor, usually at night with ships' boats, and bringing her out of harbour. Boulogne was a failed attempt.
Blockade
Keeping an enemy fleet shut in port. A close blockade stays in sight of the harbour; Nelson off Toulon and Cádiz kept an open one, out of sight, hoping the enemy would come out.
Dismasted
With masts shot away. A dismasted ship cannot move or steer and is usually finished.
Cable, fathom, knot
A cable's length is about 200 yards, a tenth of a nautical mile. A fathom is six feet, for measuring depth. A knot is one nautical mile an hour; a nautical mile is 2,025 yards, a little more than a land mile.

Guns and shot

Pounder
Guns were named by the weight of the iron ball they fired. Victory's heaviest were 32-pounders.
Carronade
A short, light gun throwing a very heavy ball a short distance; named after the Carron ironworks in Scotland. Devastating at close range, and a British speciality. Carronades were not counted in a ship's rated number of guns.
Round shot
A solid iron ball, for smashing hulls. Double-shotted or treble-shotted guns were loaded with two or three for the first broadside at close range.
Grape, canister
Bags or tins of small balls that scatter like a shotgun blast; used against men on deck and in boats.
Chain shot, bar shot
Two half-balls joined by a chain or bar, for cutting rigging and bringing down masts.
Langridge
Scrap iron fired to tear sails and rigging. A piece of it opened Nelson's forehead at the Nile.
Chasers
The few guns that could fire ahead (bow-chasers) or astern (stern-chasers).

Kinds of ship

Ship of the line
A warship strong enough to stand in the line of battle: in Nelson's time, one of 64 guns or more on at least two decks. Also “line-of-battle ship” or simply “a sail of the line”.
Rate
The Navy's classification by number of guns. First-rate: 100 or more on three decks. Second-rate: 90 to 98, three decks. Third-rate: 64 to 80, two decks. Fourth-rate: 50 to 60. Fifth- and sixth-rates were frigates and smaller.
Seventy-four
A two-decked third-rate of 74 guns: the standard ship of the line, a balance of firepower, speed and cost. Thirteen of Nelson's fourteen ships at the Nile were 74s.
Three-decker
A first- or second-rate, with three full gun decks. Tall, powerful, slow, and usually an admiral's flagship.
Frigate
A fast ship with a single gun deck and 28 to 44 guns. Frigates scouted, carried dispatches and repeated signals, and by convention were not fired on by ships of the line unless they fired first.
Sloop, brig, cutter
Small warships of twenty guns or fewer. A brig has two masts; a cutter one.
Bomb vessel
A small, strongly built ship carrying heavy mortars to lob explosive shells at targets ashore.
Blockship, hulk
An old ship with her masts removed, moored as a floating battery. Most of the Danish line at Copenhagen.
Flagship
The ship an admiral is in, flying his flag.

Parts of a ship

See them on the deck plan of HMS Victory.

Quarterdeck
The raised deck aft of the mainmast, from which the ship was commanded. Officers' territory. Nelson was shot on Victory's.
Poop
The short, highest deck right aft, above the quarterdeck.
Forecastle
The raised deck at the bow. Said “fo'c'sle”.
Gun deck
A deck carrying a full row of guns. A three-decker had a lower, middle and upper gun deck; the heaviest guns were lowest.
Orlop
The lowest deck, below the waterline, safe from shot. It held stores, the cable tiers and the cockpit.
Cockpit
The space on the orlop where the midshipmen lived, used in battle as the surgeon's operating room. Nelson died in Victory's.
Fore, main, mizzen
The three masts, from bow to stern. Each was built in sections: lower mast, topmast, topgallant mast.
Yard
A spar crossing a mast, from which a square sail hangs. Its ends are the yardarms.
Top
A platform partway up a mast. In battle, marksmen were posted in the tops. The ball that killed Nelson came from the Redoutable's mizzen-top.
Colours, ensign
The national flag flown by a ship. A British fleet flew the Red, White or Blue Ensign according to its admiral's squadron.

People and ranks

Midshipman
A boy or young man training to be an officer. Nelson became one at twelve.
Lieutenant
The first commissioned rank, reached by examination after at least six years at sea. A ship of the line had from three to eight; the first lieutenant was second-in-command.
Commander
Captain of a vessel too small for a post-captain, such as a brig or sloop.
Post-captain
A captain appointed to a ship of twenty guns or more. “Being made post” was the decisive step: from then on an officer rose towards admiral by seniority alone, as those above him died or were promoted.
Commodore
A senior captain given temporary command of a squadron. He flew a swallow-tailed broad pendant instead of an admiral's flag.
Flag officer
An admiral. There were three grades, rear-admiral, vice-admiral and admiral, and within each grade three squadrons in rising order: Blue, White, Red. Nelson died a Vice-Admiral of the White.
Flag-captain
The captain of a flagship, who ran the ship while the admiral ran the fleet. Hardy to Nelson.
Captain of the fleet
A senior officer acting as chief of staff to a commander-in-chief.
Master
The warrant officer responsible for navigation and the handling of the ship.
Coxswain
The seaman in charge of a boat and its crew. An admiral's coxswain was a picked man.
Half-pay
The retainer paid to an officer without a ship. Nelson lived on it from 1787 to 1793.
Pressed man
A seaman taken into the Navy by force by a press gang. Perhaps half of a wartime crew.
Marines
Soldiers serving in ships, about one man in six of a crew. “Royal” from 1802.

Paper

Signal book
The printed code in which numbered flags stood for standard orders: No. 16 was “engage the enemy more closely”, No. 39 “discontinue the action”. Popham's Telegraphic Signals, added in 1803, let an admiral spell out any sentence.
Fighting Instructions
The Navy's standing orders for battle, which required captains to keep their place in the line. Leaving it without orders, as Nelson did at Cape St Vincent, was a court-martial offence.
Dispatch
A commander's official report of an action, sent to the Admiralty and printed in the London Gazette. To be named in it was an honour; to be left out was noticed.
Parole, cartel
A prisoner's promise not to fight again until formally exchanged; and the agreement, or the ship, by which prisoners were exchanged.
Log
The ship's official journal of courses, winds and events, kept by the master and the officers. The source of most of what is known about the battles, and of most of the disagreements about timing.
