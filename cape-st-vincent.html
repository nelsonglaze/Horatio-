Battles  ·  14 February 1797

The Battle of Cape St Vincent

Sir John Jervis takes fifteen ships through the middle of a Spanish fleet of twenty-seven. When the larger half looks like escaping, Commodore Nelson turns out of the line on his own judgement, blocks its path with a single 74, and ends the day by boarding one enemy ship across the deck of another.

Off Cape St Vincent, south-west Portugal  ·  about 37°N 9°W

Date

“A victory is very essential to England”

The winter of 1796 was the low point of the war for Britain. Spain had changed sides and joined France. Bonaparte had overrun northern Italy. The Navy had been ordered out of the Mediterranean altogether, and the last job in it, bringing away the garrison of Elba, fell to Commodore Nelson in the frigate Minerve.

On 1 February the Spanish Grand Fleet under Don José de Córdoba left Cartagena to join the French at Brest for an invasion. An easterly gale blew it through the Strait and far out into the Atlantic. Nelson, hurrying west from Gibraltar to rejoin Jervis, sailed straight through it in fog on the night of the 11th, close enough to count ships and too close to be suspected. He reached the fleet on the 13th, reported, and shifted his broad pendant back into the 74-gun Captain.

Jervis had fifteen of the line. At dawn on the 14th, in haze, the enemy came in sight to the south, running east for Cádiz before the wind, and the count began to arrive on Victory's quarterdeck.

“There are eight sail of the line, Sir John.” “Very well, sir.” “There are twenty sail of the line, Sir John.” “Very well, sir.” “There are twenty-seven sail of the line, Sir John.” “Enough, sir, no more of that. The die is cast, and if there are fifty sail I will go through them.”

The odds were less wild than they looked. Córdoba's ships were magnificent and badly manned: most had only sixty to eighty trained seamen, the rest being soldiers and pressed landsmen who had hardly been to sea. And the Spanish fleet was in no order at all. Eighteen ships straggled in one mass to windward; nine more were several miles off to leeward. Jervis formed a single line and steered for the gap.

The slow manoeuvre and the quick one

Having cut the Spaniards in two, Jervis signalled his line to tack in succession and go after the larger half. Tacking in succession kept the line intact, but every ship had to sail to the spot where the leader had turned before turning herself. For the ships at the rear that meant another half-hour on the wrong course, away from the enemy.

Córdoba saw his chance. The head of his main body put up its helm and steered to pass behind the British rear, rejoin the leeward ships and run for Cádiz. From the third ship from the end of the line, Nelson watched them do it. He did not wait to reach the turning-point. He ordered Captain to wear, turning away from the wind in a tight circle, cut back through his own line between the two ships astern of him, and steered straight for the leading Spaniards.

There was a signal flying that could be read as giving him leave: number 41, to take suitable stations for mutual support and engage as arriving up in succession, had been made to the rear division, and its admiral had done nothing about it. But Nelson had left the line of battle without orders, which by the Fighting Instructions was a court-martial matter. Jervis saw what he was doing and at once ordered Excellent to follow him.

Nelson's patent bridge

For most of an hour Captain fought the head of the Spanish fleet with only Culloden near her. By half past three she had lost her fore-topmast and her wheel, and not a sail or rope was left whole. Collingwood's Excellent, passing between her and the 80-gun San Nicolás at ten feet, fired a broadside that drove the Spaniard up into the wind and foul of the three-decker San Josef. Captain could no longer steer. Nelson had her laid against San Nicolás's quarter and called for boarders.

A soldier of the 69th Regiment, serving aboard as a marine, smashed the upper quarter-gallery window with his musket and Nelson went through it after him. Captain Berry was already on the poop hauling down the ensign. San Josef opened fire on them with muskets from her stern gallery above. Nelson posted sentries on the hatches, called for more men, and led them up the main-chains of the first-rate. A Spanish officer leaned over the rail to say she surrendered.

On her quarterdeck, Nelson wrote, “extravagant as the story may seem, did I receive the swords of vanquished Spaniards; which, as I received, I gave to William Fearney, one of my bargemen, who put them, with the greatest sang-froid, under his arm.” Victory, passing, gave three cheers, and so did every ship behind her.

How to read the plan

North is at the top; the wind blows from the left. The Spanish fleet was a crowd, not a line, and no two accounts place its ships alike. The positions of the lesser Spanish ships are indicative only.

Sea terms are explained in the glossary.

Tack or wear

To tack is to turn a ship's bows through the wind; to wear is to turn her stern through it. Wearing loses ground to leeward but is quick and sure, even with damaged rigging. Nelson wanted to go to leeward.

Calder's complaint

That evening Jervis's captain of the fleet, Robert Calder, remarked that Nelson's turn had been a breach of orders. “It certainly was so,” said Jervis, “and if ever you commit such a breach of your orders, I will forgive you also.”

The day, hour by hour

  1. 6.30 am

    Culloden signals five sail in the south-west. The count climbs through the morning as the haze lifts.

  2. 11.00 am

    Jervis signals: form line of battle ahead and astern of Victory as most convenient. Then: the admiral intends to pass through the enemy's line.

  3. 11.30 am

    Culloden opens fire as she passes between the two Spanish bodies. A collision with a Spanish three-decker looks certain; “Let the weakest fend off,” says Troubridge, and the Spaniard gives way.

  4. 12.08 pm

    Signal to tack in succession. Culloden's answering flag breaks out before Victory's signal is fully up. “Look at Troubridge!” says Jervis. “He tacks his ship in battle as if the eyes of England were upon him.”

  5. 12.30 pm

    The Spanish leeward division tries to cut through at the turning-point. Victory rakes Príncipe de Asturias; the Spaniards sheer off.

  6. 12.50 pm

    The Spanish van bears up to pass astern of the British line. Nelson wears Captain out of the line.

  7. 1.10 pm

    Captain opens fire on Santísima Trinidad. Culloden is coming up; Jervis signals Excellent to leave the line.

  8. 2.15 pm

    Excellent engages Salvador del Mundo, which strikes, and moves on.

  9. 2.50 pm

    San Ysidro hauls down her colours to Excellent.

  10. 3.20 pm

    San Nicolás and San Josef fall aboard each other. Captain is laid alongside and Nelson boards.

  11. 4.00 pm

    San Josef surrenders. Santísima Trinidad, with Blenheim, Orion, Irresistible and Excellent round her, is seen to haul down her colours.

  12. 4.20 pm

    The Spanish leeward division and two fresh ships come up. Jervis signals the fleet to form line and cover the prizes. Santísima Trinidad re-hoists her flag and is towed away.

What it won

Four prizes out of twenty-seven was not annihilation, and Jervis has been criticised for not renewing the action next day against a beaten enemy. But he had got what he said was essential. The Spanish fleet went into Cádiz and was blockaded there; the junction with the French at Brest never happened. In a year that brought a run on the Bank of England and mutiny in the fleets at Spithead and the Nore, it was the only good news.

Jervis was made Earl of St Vincent. Nelson, who had already been promoted rear-admiral by seniority before the news reached London, was made a Knight of the Bath, which he preferred to the baronetcy first suggested, since a red ribbon and star could be worn. Córdoba was court-martialled and dismissed.

Nelson had taken a heavy blow in the stomach from a flying block, which left him with a hernia for life. He also made sure the story was told. His own account, A Few Remarks Relative to Myself in the Captain, was in the London papers before Jervis's dispatch, which had not mentioned him by name. The public learned a new name and the fleet a new joke: “Nelson's Patent Bridge for Boarding First-Rates”.

“Westminster Abbey, or glorious victory!”
Nelson's reported cry as he led the boarders into San Josef
“By God, I'll not lose Hardy”

Three days before the battle, with Spanish ships chasing Minerve out of Gibraltar, a man fell overboard. Lieutenant Hardy went after him in the jolly-boat and was being left behind. Nelson backed his topsail to wait for him. The leading Spaniard, unable to believe a frigate would stop unless she had support in sight, shortened sail too, and Minerve got away.
