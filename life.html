The man, 1758–1805

Life & upbringing

He was a clergyman's sixth child from a flat corner of Norfolk, small and often ill, who lost his mother at nine and left home for good at twelve. He spoke with a Norfolk accent all his life and was seasick at the start of every voyage.

Burnham Thorpe, Norfolk  ·  52°56′N 0°45′E

The parsonage

Horatio Nelson was born on 29 September 1758 in the rectory at Burnham Thorpe, a village of a few hundred people two miles from the North Sea. He was so frail that he was baptised privately at ten days old, in case he did not last until the public christening in November. At home he was called Horace.

His father, the Reverend Edmund Nelson, held the living and several neighbouring ones. He was a mild, devout, anxious man with a weak chest, who spent his winters at Bath and worried about money. His mother, Catherine Suckling, came from a better-connected family: her grandmother was a sister of Sir Robert Walpole, the first Prime Minister, and the boy was named after his godfather, Lord Walpole. The Walpoles were the great people of the county and took little notice of their poor relations at the rectory.

Catherine bore eleven children in seventeen years and buried three of them. She died on Boxing Day 1767, aged forty-two, leaving eight, the youngest ten months old. Horatio was nine. He said little about her afterwards except that she hated the French; but nearly forty years on he wrote that “the thought of former days brings all my mother into my heart, which shows itself in my eyes”.

School

The rector could not manage eight children and a parish, and sent the older boys away. Horatio and his brother William boarded at the Royal Grammar School in Norwich and then at Sir William Paston's School at North Walsham, where the master was a noted flogger. The stories told of his boyhood all make the same point and may have been improved in the telling. Lost for hours as a small child and asked by his grandmother whether fear had not driven him home, he is supposed to have said, “Fear? I never saw fear. What is it?” At North Walsham he was lowered from the dormitory window on knotted sheets to strip the headmaster's pear tree, gave all the pears away, and said he had only done it because every other boy was afraid.

“What has poor Horatio done?”

In the Christmas holidays of 1770 the boys read in the county newspaper that their mother's brother, Captain Maurice Suckling, had been given the 64-gun Raisonnable. A war with Spain over the Falkland Islands was expected. Horatio, twelve years old, asked William to write to their father at Bath: he wanted to go to sea with his uncle. The rector passed the request on, and Suckling replied in the tone of a man humouring a relative.

“What has poor Horatio done, who is so weak, that he, above all the rest, should be sent to rough it out at sea? But let him come; and the first time we go into action, a cannon-ball may knock off his head, and provide for him at once.”
Captain Maurice Suckling to the Reverend Edmund Nelson, 1771

His father put him on the London coach and then the Chatham stage, and left him there. Nobody met him. He walked about the dockyard in the cold until an officer who knew his uncle took pity on him, found Raisonnable out at her moorings, and learnt on getting aboard that Captain Suckling was away and nobody expected a new midshipman. He paced the deck by himself for the rest of the day. It was a start he never forgot, and as an admiral he took trouble over frightened boys.

What his uncle did for him

The Spanish war did not come and Raisonnable paid off. Suckling, who became Comptroller of the Navy, then managed his nephew's education with unusual care. He sent him to the West Indies in a merchant ship to learn seamanship before the mast; gave him the guardship's boats on the Thames and Medway until he was “a good pilot for vessels of that description”; got him into an Arctic expedition at fourteen, though boys were barred; and then on a two-year voyage to India. By the time Nelson passed for lieutenant in April 1777, with his uncle on the examining board saying nothing about the relationship until it was over, he had seen the tropics, the ice and the Indian Ocean. Suckling died the next year. Nelson was a post-captain at twenty, an age at which no later influence could hold him back.

The family

Of the eleven children, eight grew up: Maurice, a clerk at the Navy Office; Susannah, who married Thomas Bolton; William, a clergyman, who inherited the titles and was made an earl; Horatio; Ann; Edmund; Suckling; and Catherine, the baby, who married George Matcham and lived until 1842.

What he was like

Estimates of his height run from five feet four to five feet six, not small for his day, but he was very slight and looked smaller. His hair was sandy and went white early. He lost most of his upper teeth. People meeting him for the first time were usually disappointed; Emma Hamilton's husband thought him at first sight a little man who could not boast of being very handsome, and Prince William remembered “the merest boy of a captain I ever beheld”, in an old-fashioned waistcoat with his hair in a stiff tail of extraordinary length.

Then he began to talk. Almost everyone who left an account describes the same change: an animation and directness, an enthusiasm for the service that he seemed to pass on by contact. The Duke of Wellington, who met him once by chance in a waiting-room in 1805, said that for the first quarter of an hour Nelson talked about himself in a style “so vain and so silly as to surprise and almost disgust me”, and then, on learning who Wellington was, talked for another quarter of an hour like an officer and a statesman. “I don't know that I ever had a conversation that interested me more.”

He was openly emotional, cried easily, and wanted to be loved and praised more than is comfortable to watch. He was also consistently kind to people who could do nothing for him: midshipmen, whom he called his children; old shipmates; the sick. He was the son of a parson and stayed a believer, saying his prayers night and morning. He hated the French, on principle and by upbringing, and never learnt to speak their language despite a trip to France in 1783 to try.

The vision

At seventeen, invalided home from India with malaria and convinced he had no prospects, he went through a depression so black that he “almost wished himself overboard”. What followed, as he told it later, was a kind of conversion.

“A sudden glow of patriotism was kindled within me, and presented my King and Country as my patron. Well then, I exclaimed, I will be a hero, and confiding in Providence, I will brave every danger.”

He spoke for the rest of his life of a radiant orb that hung before him and drew him on.

A body used up

The tally of wounds is on the reckoning sheet. Behind them was a constitution that had never been strong: malaria from the East Indies that returned for the rest of his life, the dysentery and yellow fever that nearly killed him in Nicaragua, scurvy in 1782. After the amputation of 1797 a ligature caught on a nerve and gave him months of pain until it came away. He felt his missing fingers for the rest of his life, and said it was proof that the soul existed. And he was seasick. “I am ill every time it blows hard,” he wrote in 1804, “and nothing but my enthusiastic love for the profession keeps me one hour at sea.”

On the beach

From 1787 to 1793 he had no ship. He lived on half-pay in his father's parsonage, dug the garden, read the newspapers, and wrote to the Admiralty asking for anything, even “a cockle-boat”. He believed he was being punished for enforcing the trade laws too well in the West Indies. Five years is a long time, and he was thirty-four when war with France ended it.

Fanny

As a young captain he fell in love easily and unsuccessfully: with a sixteen-year-old in Quebec, for whom a friend had to argue him out of leaving his ship; with a clergyman's daughter at Saint-Omer, who turned him down; and with the married wife of the dockyard commissioner at Antigua, as a friend.

In 1785, on the island of Nevis, he met Frances Nisbet, a doctor's widow of about his own age with a five-year-old son, Josiah, who kept house for her rich uncle, the president of the island's council. His letters to her speak of esteem and of her good sense more than of passion. They were married at the Montpelier estate on 11 March 1787. Prince William Henry, the future William IV, then a frigate captain under Nelson's orders, gave the bride away.

Fanny was gentle, conventional and nervous, and nothing in her life had prepared her for five winters in a Norfolk parsonage with a restless husband on half-pay, or for a decade of his absence afterwards. She nursed his father devotedly. There were no children, which grieved them both. Her letters fuss about his safety; “leave boarding to captains,” she begged after St Vincent. When he came home in 1797 without his arm, she learnt to dress the stump and cut up his food, and those months were probably the happiest of the marriage.

Emma

Emma Hamilton had been born Amy Lyon, a blacksmith's daughter from Cheshire. She had been a nursemaid, an artist's model whom George Romney painted dozens of times, and the mistress of a young aristocrat who, when he needed to marry money, passed her on to his widowed uncle. The uncle was Sir William Hamilton, British envoy at Naples, a connoisseur of vases and volcanoes. He married her in 1791. By the time Nelson knew her she was the closest friend of the Queen of Naples, spoke Italian and French, and was known across Europe for her “Attitudes”, a performance in which she posed with shawls as figures from classical art.

Nelson had met the Hamiltons for a few days in 1793. In September 1798 he came back to Naples as the victor of the Nile, with a head wound, a fever and one arm. Emma came aboard, cried “Oh God, is it possible?” and fell against him. She took him home and nursed him, and gave him a birthday party for eighteen hundred guests. Within a year they were lovers, with Sir William, who was sixty-eight and fond of both, apparently content. Nelson called the three of them tria juncta in uno, three joined in one, the motto of the Order of the Bath.

They travelled home together overland in 1800, a progress through Vienna, Prague and Dresden that scandalised observers. Fanny met them in London. After some weeks of his praising Lady Hamilton across the breakfast table, she said she was sick of hearing of dear Lady Hamilton, and that he must give up one or the other. “Take care, Fanny, what you say,” he answered. “I love you sincerely, but I cannot forget my obligations to Lady Hamilton.” He left the house. He settled half his income on her, wrote that he wished only to be left to himself, and never saw her again. One of her later letters came back endorsed: “Opened by mistake by Lord Nelson, but not read.”

Horatia and Merton

Emma's daughter by Nelson was born in London at the end of January 1801 and carried off within days to a nurse in Marylebone. To keep the secret, Nelson and Emma wrote to each other about a seaman called Thompson, whose wife had had a child and who was most anxious about them both. The girl was christened Horatia Nelson Thompson and presented to the world as the admiral's ward. A second daughter, born early in 1804, lived only a few weeks.

In 1801 Nelson borrowed £9,000 and bought Merton Place, a house in Surrey an hour's drive from the Admiralty. Emma filled it with pictures of him and of herself, flags, and china with his arms on it; a visitor called it a shrine. Sir William moved in too, and grumbled only about the noise. He died in April 1803 with his wife's arms round him and Nelson holding his hand. Six weeks later Nelson sailed for the Mediterranean, and was away two years and three months.

He had twenty-five days at Merton in the late summer of 1805. On the evening of 13 September he went upstairs to where Horatia was asleep, knelt by the bed and prayed, and came down to the carriage.

“Friday night, at half-past ten, drove from dear, dear Merton, where I left all which I hold dear in this world, to go to serve my King and Country.”
Private diary, 13 September 1805

What became of them

In the last hour of his life Nelson came back again and again to the same request: that Lady Hamilton and Horatia should be looked after. He had written it into a codicil to his will on the morning of the battle, leaving them “a legacy to my King and Country”. The country declined the bequest.

His brother William, a grasping clergyman he had never much liked, was made Earl Nelson with £5,000 a year and £99,000 to buy an estate. His sisters had £15,000 each. Fanny, the lawful widow, had a pension of £2,000. Emma had what Nelson had been able to leave her, which was Merton and an annuity, and spent it. She was arrested for debt in 1813, got away to Calais with Horatia the next year, and died there in January 1815, a few months before Waterloo.

Nelson himself came home in a cask. His body lay in state at Greenwich, was rowed up the Thames, and on 9 January 1806 was buried under the dome of St Paul's, in the coffin made from the mast of L'Orient. The funeral and what followed are on the legacy page.

Titles

Knight of the Bath, 1797. Baron Nelson of the Nile, 1798. Duke of Bronté in Sicily, a gift of the King of Naples, 1799; he signed himself “Nelson & Bronté” from then on. Viscount, 1801. Vice-Admiral of the White, 1804.
