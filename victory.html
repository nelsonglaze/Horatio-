HMS Victory

She was forty years old at Trafalgar and had already been the flagship of six admirals. She is still in commission, the oldest warship in the world to be so, and lies in dry dock at Portsmouth where the largest repair of her life is now being carried out in public.

A ship named in a year of victories

Victory was ordered in 1758, the year Nelson was born, and her keel was laid at Chatham in the summer of 1759, the “wonderful year” of Minden, Quebec and Quiberon Bay. The name was chosen despite its record: the last Victory had gone down with all eleven hundred hands in 1744.

Her lines were drawn by Thomas Slade, the best naval architect of his century, and they turned out unusually well. For a three-decker she was fast and weatherly, able to keep up with a fleet of two-deckers, which is why admirals kept asking for her. She was floated out of the dock in May 1765, when it was found that her gunport sills were a little lower than designed, and then, since the war had ended, she lay in reserve on the Medway for thirteen years.

She was first commissioned in 1778, for the American war, and from then on was almost always somebody's flagship: Keppel's in the muddled battle off Ushant in 1778, Kempenfelt's when he cut out a French convoy in 1781, Howe's at the relief of Gibraltar in 1782, Hood's at Toulon in 1793, and Jervis's at Cape St Vincent in 1797, where Nelson watched her from the Captain.

By the end of 1797 she was judged worn out and was fitted as a hospital ship for prisoners of war. The loss of another first-rate reprieved her. Between 1800 and 1803 she was rebuilt at Chatham at a cost greater than her building: the open stern galleries were closed in, the figurehead replaced, and she was painted in the black and yellow bands, with black port-lids, that Nelson's ships made famous. She was recommissioned in April 1803 and Nelson hoisted his flag in her a month later.

At Trafalgar

On 21 October 1805 she had 821 people aboard, including thirty-one boys, the youngest twelve years old. They came from all over the British Isles and from some twenty other countries. She led the weather column into the Combined Fleet, was under fire for forty minutes before she could reply, raked Bucentaure, and locked yards with Redoutable. She fired her guns on both sides at once for much of the afternoon.

Fifty-seven of her people were killed and 102 wounded. Her mizzen-mast went over the side, her wheel was shot away so that she had to be steered by forty men hauling on tackles in the gunroom, and her sails were in ribbons. Nelson fell on the quarterdeck, at a spot now marked by a brass plate, and died on the orlop deck below the waterline.

Each weighed about three tons with its carriage and needed a crew of up to fourteen. A good crew could load, run out and fire in ninety seconds. The ball would go through two feet of oak at close range.

Two hundred and sixty years

  1. 1759

    Keel laid at Chatham, 23 July.

  2. 1765

    Floated out, 7 May. Laid up in reserve.

  3. 1778

    First commissioned. Flagship of Admiral Keppel at the first battle of Ushant.

  4. 1781–82

    Flagship of Kempenfelt at the second battle of Ushant, and of Lord Howe at the relief of Gibraltar and off Cape Spartel.

  5. 1793–94

    Lord Hood's flagship at Toulon and Corsica. Captain Nelson of the Agamemnon reports aboard her.

  6. 1797

    Sir John Jervis's flagship at Cape St Vincent. Paid off later that year as unfit and made a hospital ship.

  7. 1800–03

    The “great repair” at Chatham. Recommissioned April 1803; Nelson's flag hoisted 18 May.

  8. 1805

    Trafalgar, 21 October. Towed to Gibraltar; sails for England with Nelson's body on 4 November and reaches Spithead on 4 December.

  9. 1808–12

    Flagship of Sir James Saumarez in the Baltic. Brings troops home from Corunna in 1809. Paid off for the last time as a sea-going ship in 1812.

  10. 1831

    Listed for breaking up. By tradition Sir Thomas Hardy, then First Sea Lord, went home, told his wife what he had signed, and was sent back the next morning to unsign it.

  11. 1889

    Becomes flagship of the Commander-in-Chief at Portsmouth, moored in the harbour.

  12. 1903

    Rammed at her moorings by an old ironclad breaking adrift under tow, and saved from sinking only by emergency docking.

  13. 1922

    After a public appeal led by the Society for Nautical Research, moved into No. 2 Dry Dock on 12 January, never to float again. Restoration to her 1805 appearance begins.

  14. 1928

    Opened to the public by King George V, 17 July.

  15. 1941

    A German bomb bursts in the dock under her bow and blows a hole in the hull.

  16. 2005

    Centrepiece of the Trafalgar bicentenary.

  17. 2012

    Ownership passes from the Ministry of Defence to a preservation trust within the National Museum of the Royal Navy. She becomes flagship of the First Sea Lord.

  18. 2020

    The cradles she had sat on since the 1920s are replaced by 134 adjustable steel props that spread her weight as water once did.

  19. 2022–

    The Big Repair.

The Big Repair

A wooden ship is built to be held up by water pressing evenly on every part of her hull. Standing in a dry dock for a century, Victory has been slowly sagging: her sides bulging outward, her keel distorting, the whole structure settling by fractions of an inch each year. At the same time rainwater has been finding its way into the hull, and where oak stays damp it rots and is attacked by fungus and beetle.

The new props, finished in 2020, dealt with the first problem. The second needs the ship taken apart from the outside. In the project the museum calls HMS Victory: The Big Repair, shipwrights are removing the outer planking from the hull, section by section, repairing or replacing the rotten framing behind it, and then re-planking the ship in new oak. The museum has described it as a project of at least ten years costing about £42 million, the most extensive work on her since the 1920s, with completion expected in the early 2030s.

To keep the weather out while she is open, the ship has been enclosed in a scaffold building. Her masts have been lifted out, and the museum notes it is the first time she has stood without any since the 1890s. She stays open through all of it. Visitors go aboard as before, and can also walk the scaffold galleries alongside the hull and watch the shipwrights and conservators at work on timbers that have not been seen since the eighteenth century.

The work is also a survey. Each plank that comes off is recorded and the timber behind it examined and dated, building up the first full picture of which parts of the ship fought at Trafalgar and which were renewed in the repairs since.

Still in commission

Victory has a serving commanding officer and a ship's company, flies the White Ensign, and is the flagship of the First Sea Lord. The United States frigate Constitution, launched in 1797, is the oldest commissioned warship still afloat; Victory is thirty-two years older.

Going aboard

Victory is at Portsmouth Historic Dockyard, a few minutes' walk from Portsmouth Harbour railway station, alongside the Mary Rose, HMS Warrior and the museum's galleries. The route through the ship follows the decks downwards: the great cabin where the captains heard the plan, the quarterdeck and the plate where Nelson fell, the three gun decks with the mess tables slung between the guns, and finally the orlop and the cockpit, lit by lanterns, where a wreath marks the place he died.

The headroom on the gun decks is well under six feet. Most of the crew ate, slept and fought there.

What to look for

  • The fore-topsail she carried at Trafalgar, holed by some ninety shot, survives in the museum's care.
  • The barge that carried Nelson's coffin up the Thames is in the museum beside the dock.
  • Each year on 21 October the ship flies the “England expects” signal, and the Navy drinks a toast in silence to the Immortal Memory.
