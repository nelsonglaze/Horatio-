The lopsidedness has causes. British crews fired faster and aimed at hulls; French and Spanish practice was to fire high at masts and rigging, which disabled ships but killed fewer men. Raking fire at the moment the line was broken did terrible damage in seconds. And a ship that fights on until she is dismasted, as most of these did, loses far more than one that is allowed to sail away.

The rest of the account

Other captures he had a hand in

  • Ça Ira (80) and Censeur (74), March 1795. Agamemnon crippled the first; both were taken next day by Hotham's fleet.
  • Alcide (74), July 1795. Struck off the Hyères Islands with Agamemnon among her attackers, then caught fire and blew up.
  • Santa Sabina (40), December 1796. Taken by Minerve after three hours, and retaken by a Spanish squadron next morning.
  • Généreux (74), February 1800, to his own flagship; and Guillaume Tell (80), March 1800, to his squadron while he was ashore.
  • In Corsica in 1794, the towns of Bastia and Calvi, with the warships in their harbours.

The debit side

  • Tenerife, July 1797: about 250 killed, drowned and wounded, the cutter Fox sunk with 97 men, and nothing gained.
  • Boulogne, August 1801: 44 killed and 128 wounded in a boat attack that did not take a single vessel.
  • Turks Island, March 1783: a small landing beaten off.
  • Leander (50): taken by the escaping Généreux eighteen days after the Nile, carrying the dispatches home.

His own account, 1797

After Tenerife, a one-armed rear-admiral had to apply for his pension in writing, stating his services. Nelson's memorial to the King is the nearest thing to a balance sheet he ever drew up, and it was written before any of his three great victories.

“In four actions with the fleets of the enemy, in three actions with frigates, in six engagements against batteries, in ten actions in boats employed in cutting out of harbours, in destroying vessels, and in taking three towns. He has assisted at the capture of seven sail of the line, six frigates, four corvettes, and eleven privateers; taken and destroyed near fifty sail of merchant vessels; and actually been engaged against the enemy upwards of one hundred and twenty times.”
Memorial to George III, October 1797, abridged. He was granted £1,000 a year.

Captains killed under his command

George Westcott of the Majestic at the Nile. James Mosse of the Monarch and Edward Riou of the Amazon at Copenhagen. George Duff of the Mars and John Cooke of the Bellerophon at Trafalgar. Five in three battles, against at least fifteen enemy flag officers and captains killed.

Ships that changed sides

Many prizes served on in the Royal Navy. San Josef kept her name. Franklin became Canopus, Aquilon became Aboukir, Holsteen became Holstein. Tonnant and Spartiate, taken at the Nile, fought in the British line at Trafalgar. It worked both ways: Berwick and Swiftsure, in the French line that day, had both been British ships.

Why the figures differ

British captains sent in a signed return of killed and wounded, usually within days, and those are the figures used here. Men who died of wounds later were often added to one list and not another. French, Spanish and Danish losses were reported by ship where the ship survived, and guessed where she sank, burnt or was wrecked. See the sources.
